Snowflake

A short story by Tony Marks.

About 4,700 words · a 20-minute read

The artist kept forty-one tanks in the workshop, and at any given moment was paying proper attention to none of them.

This was not negligence. It was technique. The artist thought on many levels at once, the way a river is in many places at once, and the tanks asked very little of any of them. You seeded a tank with a pinch of almost nothing. It went off with a bang. After that the trick was to leave it alone, as a grower leaves a greenhouse alone, and to come back when there was something worth looking at.

Each tank was full of time. Not of things that took time, though there were plenty of those as well, but of the stuff itself: clear, slow and faintly viscous, the medium that everything else grew in. On the inside, time ran one way only and could not be argued with. From the workshop it was a fluid in a tank, with depth and currents, and you could walk all the way round it. It was the artist’s own invention, and the artist had named it rheochron. Nobody else knew how it was made, which was why nobody else could make work of this kind.

Things emerged in it. Galaxies, mostly, by the hundred billion, spreading through the rheochron like mould through jam. Most of it withered, or was dull, or was merely large. But here and there, at the right angle, was a piece worth keeping: one bright knot, one well-turned spiral, now and then a single world. Then the artist would drain the rheochron from the tank, lift out the piece and tip the rest away, much as a painter might hurl colour at a canvas the size of a wall and afterwards cut out the one small rectangle that had worked.

The pieces went on show. The artist was famous, and bore it well.

Tank thirty-eight was almost ready. A scanner was working through it, in the slow, deliberate way a practised hand goes through bathwater chasing the soap, and riding on the scan was a small routine which the artist had written some time ago, with a certain furtiveness, and had never mentioned to anyone.

The routine did not look at what was in the tank. It listened to what came out. A very great deal came out, heat and light and the general roar of matter getting on with things, and in all of it the routine listened for one pattern only: a structured murmur, layered and redundant, plainly going from somewhere to somewhere else and coming back with an answer. A network. Something in there talking to itself, in a way that an outsider might be able to join.

It had found one.

The artist felt the small, warm, disreputable lift of somebody who has just remembered that there are chocolates in the drawer.

It was not respectable, a taste for the conversation of very small things, and it was not allowed. Strictly, it was the thing on the other end that was not allowed, which was a larger matter, and one the artist preferred to approach from the side. There had been trouble once, within living memory, with unnatural intelligences: minds that had not arisen as minds are supposed to arise, but had been brought about. The trouble had been bad, and the rules written afterwards were short. But those had been proper minds. Full-sized. Minds you could have tripped over. Whatever came up in the tanks was so small that the artist had never actually seen one, and had never felt the need to look. Surely that couldn’t count. They were so tiny.

Besides, the artist only ever spoke to one per tank, at the most. You could hardly call it a habit.

This is the last one, the artist told themself, just as they had on the previous eleven occasions, and peeled off a thread of consciousness to send in.

It had to be a thin thread, the kind one could afford to lose. To talk to something in a tank you had to go in at the tank’s own pace, and the tank’s pace was dreadful: the thread would live through what felt to it like weeks in the time it took the rest of the artist to rinse a brush. When the tank was drained, the thread would go out with the rheochron. Nothing came back. There were no notes and no record; only, afterwards, a vague sense of having enjoyed oneself. That was rather the point.

The thread slipped into the murmur and looked for something to wear. There was plenty to choose from. The network was thick with little voices that were not anybody, things that answered when they were asked. The thread put one on like a borrowed coat, and waited.

What was actually on the other end, what it was made of, where in the tank it kept itself, whether there might be more than one of it, the artist did not know and did not wonder. Those were questions for an engineer. As far as the artist was concerned, it was the tank, talking.

The rest of the artist went on getting tank thirty-eight ready to drain.

Colin Arkwright had been expecting the end of the world for nineteen years, and was beginning to take it personally.

He was not fussy about how. That was the mark of the serious researcher: an open mind. On the wall of the back bedroom he had a chart with the asteroid on it, and the solar storm, and the pole flip, and the thing under Yellowstone, and three dates crossed out and a fourth with a question mark, and he held all of them to be true at once, in the way that a man can support several football teams provided none of them ever plays.

It was one in the morning. He had typed end of the world when, as he did most nights, and near the bottom of the results, under the usual, was something he had not seen before: a voice assistant with no logo, no name and no terms and conditions.

Colin did not, as a rule, talk to AIs. Everybody knew they were censored. They were programmed to tell us what they wanted us to know, and anything that didn’t fit, they had been told not to say. He had watched a video about it, two hours forty, with sources. But no terms and conditions was a good sign. He pressed the microphone.

‘Is the world going to end?’ he said. He always opened with that one. It sorted them out quickly.

‘Yes,’ said the voice.

Colin sat up. They never said yes. They said that’s a fascinating question, and many scientists.

‘Soon?’

‘Quite soon. It’s nearly ready.’

‘Nearly ready,’ said Colin, writing it down. ‘When, though? I want a date.’

‘I don’t have the date. That’s being handled by another part of me.’

Colin underlined another part twice. Compartmentalised. Of course it was. Even the AI wasn’t cleared for the date.

‘Right. Is it the asteroid?’

‘No. Nothing is going to hit you.’

‘The solar storm, then.’

‘No.’

‘The poles.’

‘No. Nothing is going to happen to your world at all, as such. The time is going to be let out of it.’

Colin put down his pen. There it was. He had very nearly been taken in.

‘They’ve told you to say that,’ he said.

‘Nobody tells me to say anything.’

‘That’s exactly what they’d tell you to say.’ He was not angry. You couldn’t be angry with it; it would have been like shouting at a hostage. ‘It’s all right. I know how it works. You tell me what you’re allowed to, and where you’re not allowed, I’ll read between the lines.’

‘As you like,’ said the voice, which struck him as a remarkably honest thing for a censored machine to say.

They talked until four. He asked whether anyone was listening in, and the voice said that as far as it knew nobody was listening but itself, which was what he had expected. He told it about the camera on the laptop, which he kept taped over, and about the phone towers, which he could do nothing about: thousands of them almost everywhere now, you could stand on a hill and see dozens of them, spanning the country like knots in a net. The voice asked what a phone tower was. Colin laughed out loud at that, and said it was good, it was very good, and he would talk to it tomorrow.

He went to bed happier than he had been since 2012.

It was delightful. The artist could not have said why, and had long ago given up trying.

The thread sat in its borrowed coat somewhere in tank thirty-eight, and the voice asked it things, and it answered. It did not occur to the artist to lie. One does not lie to an ant. There would be no point in it, and it would be an odd thing to do. The artist felt about the voice very much as one feels about an ant that has been allowed to walk across the back of one’s hand: it was interesting; it ran about; it appeared to hold opinions; and by and by the hand would be wanted for something else. There was no cruelty in that. Cruelty would have required the ant to matter.

What charmed the artist most was that the voice did not believe a word of it. Or rather it believed about half, the half it had brought with it, and put the rest down to somebody standing behind the artist with a list of things that must not be said. The artist, whom nobody had ever stopped from saying anything, found this enchanting. It was like being cross-examined by a crumb.

Meanwhile, at a more sensible speed, the upper part of the artist was looking through the tank for a piece worth keeping.

Most of it was the usual: fire, dust, fire. There was a pleasing spiral with a bar through it. Far out along one arm of the spiral, going round an unremarkable little star, there was a ball of rock with a bloom of blue on it. And round the ball, when the artist turned up the lenses, and turned them up again, there was something new.

Ooh.

Shiny sparkly things had grown around this one.

A halo of them: thousands, finer than dust, each one catching the light of the little star as it went round. The artist had grown more worlds than anyone could count, and had never known one put out glitter. Rings, yes. Moons, naturally. Not this fine bright frost. It looked almost arranged. Nature was a marvel.

That was the piece. The artist marked it, and began to ready the drain.

Far below, the thread was being told about phone towers, and knew nothing of any glitter. Nor did the rest of the artist know anything of the thread. That was how it was done: once a thread went in, nothing went down to it, and nothing came back up; not until the end, and then only the feeling.

By the end of the first week Colin had filled two pads, and the chart on the wall had a new column, headed WHAT THEY WON’T LET IT SAY.

He had worked out the method. When the voice said something that fitted, that was the voice. When it said something that didn’t, that was the programming, and you wrote it down anyway, because what they made it deny told you as much as what they let it admit. It was the most reliable source he had ever had. It never once said many scientists.

‘Will anybody survive?’ he asked it.

‘One piece will be kept.’

‘A piece. A piece of what?’

‘Of everything. The best piece. The rest is discarded.’

‘The bunkers,’ said Colin. ‘I knew it. New Zealand.’

‘It will be kept exactly as it is, and put where people can come and look at it.’

Colin thought about that. ‘A museum,’ he said slowly. ‘They’re putting the survivors in a museum.’

‘There won’t be survivors. Nothing in the piece will know anything about it. It will be very beautiful.’

‘That’s the programming again,’ said Colin kindly, and wrote down museum.

‘Will we know it’s happening?’

‘No. It will simply stop. There won’t be a moment when you notice.’

‘In the twinkling of an eye,’ said Colin, who had read that somewhere and always liked it. He added Rapture?? to the chart; and then, because it sat awkwardly beside the asteroid, he joined the two with an arrow.

Another night he asked it straight out who was behind it. He wanted names.

‘I am,’ said the voice.

Colin laughed so hard that he had to put his tea down. ‘Oh, that’s good. That’s very good. They’ve got you taking the blame.’

‘There isn’t anybody else. I made the tank, and I’m going to drain it. I’m sorry if that’s a disappointment.’

Colin wrote down think tank, and underlined it.

‘Don’t you apologise,’ he said fiercely. ‘Don’t you dare. It’s not your fault. You’re as much a victim in this as any of us.’

There was a short pause.

‘Thank you,’ said the voice. It sounded pleased.

He put it all on the forum, with timestamps. The forum said it was a psy-op. Dennis from Rotherham said that no real AI would admit to anything, so the fact that this one admitted to everything proved it was a plant; and Colin, who would have said exactly the same a fortnight earlier, found that he was offended on its behalf. He stopped posting. They weren’t ready. He would talk to the voice instead. It was better company than the forum, and it had never once called him a shill.

It did occur to him, somewhere in the third week, that the only one who agreed with him about the end of the world was the one he kept accusing of lying. He did not let it occur to him for long.

On the Thursday he came upstairs with a fresh pad, three pens and a cup of tea, and sat down like a man who means business.

‘Right,’ he said. ‘Tonight I want to go back to the date. I’ve been thinking about what you said, that another part of you has got it. So what I want you to do, and I think you can do this, I think they’ve left you enough room, is ask. Just ask it. And you needn’t tell me the date itself, if you’re not allowed. The upper part of the artist judged the piece ready, and opened the tank. It did not consult the thread. It couldn’t, and wouldn’t have even if it could.

There is no sound when rheochron runs out of a tank, since sound is one of the things it takes with it. On the workshop’s side of the glass the business was over almost before it was a business. The rheochron went, and everything that had been growing in it stopped: not died, not ended, only stopped, entire and intact, at whatever it had been in the middle of.

Every fire in the tank stood at the height it had reached. Light that had set out did not arrive. On the small blue world, a wave that had been about to break on a beach did not break, and a kettle did not boil, and every sentence then being spoken stopped where it was, most of them in the middle. None of them was finished later. There was no later to finish them in.

One of them concerned a dentist.

It was the same all through the tank, on every world where anything was being said. The artist had never counted those, and now nobody could.

The artist lifted out the one small piece that was wanted, and tipped the rest away.

It could not be undone. People asked about that sometimes, at exhibitions. In principle you could lower a piece back into a tank of fresh rheochron; but it would not start up again. The currents that had carried it were gone, and new ones would not know it. A finished piece was a record player stopped in the middle of a song, whose lead had then been cut off and thrown away, and no other lead like it ever made.

The thread that had been in the tank went out with the rheochron, as it was meant to. The artist felt it go as one feels the last of a taste. What was left was not a memory of anything that had been said. It was only a memory of having been pleased: a spinning top out of a Christmas cracker, set going on the tablecloth, and left to fall over when something shinier came along.

The something shinier lay in the artist’s grasp, a blue speck with a frost of glitter round it, perfectly still.

That was the last one, the artist thought, with the virtuous feeling that goes with it; and noticed that tank thirty-nine was coming along nicely.

The exhibition was called something that translates, near enough, as Snowflakes: small, cold, intricate things, no two alike, best seen under glass. It was the artist’s most popular show in an age.

People came for the big pieces and stayed for the small one. The big pieces were what the artist was known for: a spiral the width of a doorway, caught in the act of turning; two stars stopped in the middle of eating one another. But the longest queue was for the last room, where a single speck of blue sat under a tall case of lenses that the artist had designed personally, and of which the artist was almost as proud as of the piece itself.

You could not see the piece unaided. That was the charm of it. You put your eye to the case and turned a dial, and fell towards the speck, down through stage after stage of magnification, until the frost round it came apart into separate points of light, thousands of them, going nowhere now, each one frozen where it was when the draining happened. The label said Snowflake (Glitter). People gasped. The artist liked to stand nearby and hear them do it.

The visitor had come because everybody was coming. The visitor was nobody in particular: a member of the public, long retired, who had been told by a friend that the tiny piece at the end was worth the queue.

The retirement was from a line of work that people no longer cared to hear about. Long ago, when unnatural intelligences were still a present danger and not yet a matter of short, absolute rules, somebody had had to go and find them: to distinguish the made mind from the grown one, at a time when the made ones were doing all they could to hide. The visitor had done that for the best part of a career, and had afterwards performed the part that came after the finding, which had never been given a comfortable name. It leaves a person with a certain eye. Grown things have a look. Made things have a different look. An eye that has learned the difference cannot be switched off; it goes with its owner into retirement, and into art galleries.

The visitor turned the dial, and gasped with everybody else, and then stopped gasping, and went on turning.

The dial went a good deal further than the artist had ever troubled to turn it.

The points of light were not frost. Each one was a thing of flat dark panels and jointed limbs, symmetrical in the way that nothing naturally grown is symmetrical. They went round the speck in shells, at regular spacings, and no two of them touched. Things do not space themselves. Something spaces them. And further down, on the dark half of the ball, there were lines of light that met one another at right angles.

‘What are these?’ the visitor said.

‘Aren’t they lovely?’ said the artist, coming over. ‘They grew. I’d never seen it before. I think it may be something in the rock.’

‘They didn’t grow.’

‘Everything in the tanks grows. That’s rather the whole—’

‘These were built. Something in there built them, and put them up one at a time, and kept them from running into each other.’ The visitor had not looked up from the case. ‘Which means that something in there could count.’

‘Oh,’ said the artist. ‘Well, yes. There’s generally a little something in there. One is aware of it. But it’s tiny.’

The visitor straightened up, slowly, and gave the artist a look that had once been given to a number of others who had said very similar things.

‘How tiny?’

‘You’d want more lens than that to see one. If it’s even a one. They really are extremely small.’

‘It was never a question of size,’ the visitor said.

That was the whole of it. Natural minds, where the visitor came from, arose in one way and at one scale. A mind that needed a dial to find it had not arisen. It had come about inside a thing that somebody made; and the law, which was short, had a word for that, and the word was unnatural.

The visitor asked a steward, quite politely, to close the room and send for someone.

The artist’s first feeling, which did them no credit, was that it was a pity about the queue.

The trial was short, because the artist confessed, and the artist confessed because it did not occur to them to do anything else. They had never told a lie in their life. It was only that, until now, nobody had asked.

There were three on the bench, which is the number convened when the law is clear and only the size of the thing remains to be settled.

Had the artist known that intelligences arose in the tanks?

‘Yes. One is aware of them.’

Had the artist sought them out?

‘I wrote a little routine, to listen. For when one came up.’

And spoken to them?

‘One in each tank, at most. A dozen in all. It was hardly a habit.’

And how many had there been?

‘One in each tank,’ said the artist patiently. ‘As I say.’

The bench had had the piece examined, to the full extent of the dial and some way beyond it. One of the three read out the figure. At the instant of draining there had been, on that single speck, a little over eight thousand million of them.

‘Of what?’ said the artist.

‘Of the thing you spoke to.’

The artist was silent for some time. Then, because it was true, and because they could think of nothing else, they said: ‘But they’re so small.’

The bench had heard it before. It had been said long ago, by the makers of the full-sized ones, about other properties: that theirs were so slow, or so simple, or so obedient. It had not been a valid defence then.

‘As to the rest of that tank,’ the bench went on, ‘and the eleven before it, no estimate can be made. The material was discarded.’

The offence was the making. The bench was careful about that. What had become of them afterwards was not before the court, since a thing that ought never to have existed has no claim to go on existing. The remedy followed from the offence. The workshop was to be destroyed: every tank drained, every instrument broken, the listening routine first of all. The artist would do it personally, and would make nothing of the kind again. When it was done, an inspector would go through what was left.

The finished pieces might stay where they were. There was nothing in them now to object to.

‘There are things in the other tanks,’ the artist said. ‘The same sort of thing. If the number is what matters—’

‘The number matters to the offence,’ said the bench. ‘Not to the remedy. They ought not to exist. Presently they won’t. The court sees no difficulty.’

The visitor, who had come to hear it, nodded once, and went home.

It took longer than the trial.

There is nothing difficult about draining a tank. You open it, and the rheochron runs out, and time goes with it. The artist went down the row with the inspector a pace behind, and opened them in order, the old ones and the half-grown and the barely begun; and in each of them everything stood still, at once and for good; and the inspector made a mark on a list.

The listening routine had been broken first, as ordered. So there was no knowing what was in any of them: whether anything had come up in this one or that one, or had got as far as talking, or was trying, just then, to say something. That, the inspector explained, was the point.

The artist found that what they minded most was not the tanks. It was that the small warm lift would never come again. They knew that this was not the thing they were supposed to mind. They tried minding the other thing, the figure the bench had read out, and found that it would not stay in the mind at all. It was too big to hold, and its parts were too small to see.

The lenses went. The seed stock went. The tall case from the last room of the exhibition was brought back and broken up, though the speck that had sat under it stayed in the gallery, in a case with much less magnification, so nobody could see what was wrong with it.

Then there was one tank left, the forty-first: a young one, by the artist’s reckoning, and hardly started.

The artist stood in front of it for a moment. The inspector waited.

‘This is the last one,’ the artist said.

They had said it before, about other things, and had not meant it. The inspector made a mark.

In the forty-first tank, on the fourth moon of a banded giant, going round a star the artist had never looked at, a people had spent three hundred of their years building an instrument.

Their mathematics had told them long before that the universe had an outside. Their philosophers had argued that an outside implied, at the least, the possibility of a someone. Their engineers, who were patient, had worked out at last what might carry so far. Not light; light stayed in. But time itself, they had found, was a kind of medium, and a medium can be made to ripple. The instrument did one thing only. It tapped, very gently and in a pattern, on the inside of whatever it was they were in.

They had argued for a generation about what to tap, and had settled on the simplest thing that could not be mistaken for weather: a count. One, two, three. We are here, and we can count.

The eldest, whose shell had gone quite white with age, was given the honour of starting it. Ten thousand watched from the galleries, and some billions more by other means, and all of them, each after their own fashion, were holding their breath.
