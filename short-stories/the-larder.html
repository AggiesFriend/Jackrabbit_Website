The Larder

A short story by Tony Marks.

About 7,900 words · a 35-minute read

Launch −2 months

The lid of pod four stood open on the test harness, and Celia had been examining the pod for long enough that Martin brought her a coffee without being asked.

"Same as three," she said, ignoring the proffered cup. "Same as one and two, now I know where to look."

He leaned in beside her. The seal ran round the rim of the pod in a continuous grey band, the colour of putty and about the width of a finger. Except where it didn't. On either side of each latch housing, for a few centimetres, the band narrowed. Not by much. Enough that, once you had seen it, you could not stop seeing it.

"Moulding?"

"Moulding. The tool doesn't fill properly round the housings. Every unit off the line's the same. The display one for the exhibition centre as well."

"Is it inside tolerance?"

"It's inside the tolerance on the drawing." She ran a gloved thumb along the seal and stopped at the narrow part, as though it might feel different there. It didn't. "The drawing was signed off before they moved the latches."

Martin took a sip of the coffee he had brought for her. "Who knows?"

"I told Gerald. Gerald told someone upstairs. Their response was 'noted'."

"Noted."

"Noted."

He did the sum anyway, because it was the kind of sum engineers do whether or not anyone has asked them to. "New tool's what, twelve weeks?"

"Fourteen. Then re-qualification." She took the coffee at last. "Window's in nine."

There was nothing to say to that, and neither of them said it. The launch window was not something anyone could negotiate with. It was a matter of where things would be in the sky.

"So we write it up properly," Martin said eventually.

"I've written it up properly. Twice." Celia set the cup down on the harness, reached past him and pressed the control on the housing. Something inside gave a solid click, then the lid began to lower under the pod's own power. The seal met the rim and flattened evenly all the way round, narrow parts and all, and a run of smaller, busier sounds followed as the latches drew it home.

They stood and looked at it for a while.

It looked fine, and that was half the problem.

Launch −40 minutes

The coverage had been running since the previous evening, and the broadcaster had deployed every available asset. Drone shots of the pad, lit from below like a cathedral. A graphic of the ship, turning slowly in one corner of the screen. A clock counting down in the other. Along the bottom ran a band of text that nobody read, listing the governments and companies that had paid for it all.

Launch had been set for the small hours, partly for the trajectory and partly, the commentator admitted, so that the ground crew could work outside without fear of heat exhaustion. Even now the edges of the picture shimmered.

"For anyone just joining us," he said, in the voice of a man who had said it several times already and expected to say it several more, "the crew have been aboard since yesterday afternoon, and asleep since yesterday evening. They won't see any of this. Which is a shame, because frankly it's a spectacular sight."

A camera drone climbed up the length of the ship and back down again, giving the millions of viewers a close-up view of the hull of the massive rocket booster.

"It's worth reminding ourselves why they're going, because it isn't to plant a flag. A few years ago, the James Webb Space Telescope picked up something unusual from a planet orbiting a star you'd need a very good telescope and a great deal of patience to find. A signature of iridium. A great deal of iridium." He let that settle. "Now, iridium is what the new carbon plants need, and it's what we haven't got. Not enough of it, anyway. The plants take carbon dioxide out of the air, give us back the oxygen and turn the carbon into diamond. Build enough of them and you can begin to put the atmosphere right. But you can't build enough of them without the iridium, and that's where our crew come in. They go out, they find it, they bring it home, and we build the machines."

He made it sound simple. That was his job.

"This is the part that tends to confuse people, so bear with me. The crew will sleep for only a handful of years of subjective time before they're woken to begin their work. But they'll be travelling very fast indeed, and at those speeds time does something peculiar. By the time they open their eyes, many decades will have passed here on Earth." A pause. "That's one for the physicists. I just read it off my screen."

The picture cut to footage from the integration hall, filmed some weeks earlier: a row of pods in a clean white room, lids open, technicians moving between them in hoods and overshoes.

"Each of the crew is in one of these. There were some concerns about the pod seals earlier in the programme, I understand, but we're told those have been fully addressed." Someone was talking in his ear, and he moved on. "And I think we can go over to the exhibition centre now."

The screen split. On the left, the launch pad. On the right, a great hall under a high roof, so full that the people at the back were standing on the benches to see. In the middle of the hall, on a low plinth, stood a single pod, identical to the ones aboard the ship, its lid raised.

It was full of food. Apples, red and green. Pears. Oranges. Grapes in loose bunches, and a bowl of cherries. Tomatoes, and potatoes with the soil brushed off them. A sheaf of wheat, a cob of sweetcorn, a dish of kidney beans, a heap of rice, a punnet of strawberries. And at one end, a steak, a pair of pork chops and a coil of sausages.

"They're calling it the Larder," said the commentator. "It was the organisers' idea. Everything in there depends on a climate we're losing. The strawberries and the cherries had to be grown under glass and flown in, because there aren't any to be had outdoors this year. The meat is real, I'm told, in the sense that it once had legs, and there are very few herds left to supply the next lot. Make of that what you will." He paused. "The larder stays sealed until the crew come home. The next time anyone opens it, the idea is that it'll be in a world that can grow all of this again. A promise, if you like. Kept in the cold."

The clock in the corner ran down through the last few minutes. The commentator, to his credit, mostly stopped talking.

At zero the pad vanished into blinding light, and the sound arrived seconds later and went on arriving. On the right of the screen a technician reached behind the larder and pressed a button. The lid lowered itself, without hurry, until it met the rim and the latches took it. A faint mist bloomed behind the glass panel in the lid, hung there, and cleared. The contents lay exactly as before. Only now they would stay that way.

In the hall, where there was nothing to watch but a closed box with a glass panel in the lid, the crowd cheered anyway.

The broadcaster stayed with the ship until it was a star, and then a guess, and then nothing at all. After that, for want of anything else, the director cut back to the larder and held it there: apples, oranges, a heap of rice, a dish of beans, now visible through the glass of the lid, all of it perfect, all of it still.

It made better television than an empty sky. The camera covering the pod was set to continue streaming for anyone in the world to see.

Launch +10 years

The ceremony had been set for the evening, when the heat eased enough for people to stand outside, and the coverage had been running since late afternoon. There was one drone this time. It kept losing its picture over the crowd, so the director mostly left it parked on the roof, looking down at the car park. The graphic of the ship was back in the corner of the screen, turning slowly as it had ten years before, with a new caption underneath: Year 10. The band of sponsors still ran along the bottom. It was shorter than it used to be.

"Good evening," said the commentator, "and welcome back." He was greyer and spoke a little more slowly, but otherwise sounded exactly the same. "Ten years. I was here the night they launched. I don't mind admitting I wasn't certain I'd be here for this."

He glanced at something off camera. "Millions of you are watching online along with several thousand lucky ticket holders inside the centre and a crowd of tens of thousands outside, who will be able to watch the ceremony on one of the external screens around the building."

The hall was full. It had been full since shortly after the doors opened, and the people who lived close enough but had not got tickets, which was most of those who had applied for them, stood shoulder to shoulder outside, watching the big screens mounted high on the outside walls of the building. There were three of them and the clear and steady picture on each relayed the ceremony from inside. Whenever the view was switched to the camera pointing at the larder, a cheer went up, as if something had happened.

In the middle of the hall, on the same low plinth, stood the larder. The lid was down as it had been for a decade, and the light shone through the glass panel onto the food. Apples, red and green. Pears. Cherries, strawberries and grapes. Oranges. Potatoes and tomatoes. A sheaf of wheat, a cob of sweetcorn, a heap of rice, a dish of kidney beans. And at one end, a steak, a pair of pork chops and a coil of sausages. Nothing had moved. Nothing had so much as darkened.

"There it is," said the commentator, quietly, the way people talk in museums. "Not a thing out of place."

The Mission Director came to the lectern promptly at eight. The operational side of the mission had been wound up years ago, once there was nothing left to operate, and the title had passed to a succession of people whose job was mostly this. She wore a lanyard nobody needed to see.

She read the names first, slowly, and the hall answered each one.

"Commander Adaeze Schneider. Rafael Castellanos. Ingrid Solheim. Tomasz Wierzbicki. Priya Raman. Kenji Moriyama. Leila Haddad. Callum Reid."

Every name got its own cheer, and the last got the loudest, because it was the last.

"They sleep a short while," she said, when the hall had settled. "We wait many lifetimes."

It was the commentator's explanation from launch night with the physics taken out, short enough to fit on a banner. There was one hanging above the doors.

Then she turned to the larder.

"Ten years ago we put these things in the cold," she said, "and we made a promise over them. I'm here to tell you that every crop in there is still being grown."

She had to wait for the applause to subside.

"Not all of them the way they used to be. The strawberries, the cherries, the grapes and the oranges grow under glass now, in houses where we keep the weather the way it was. The apples and the pears are going the same way. But they grow. We haven't lost a single one."

She let that run, too.

"The meat I won't pretend about. Some people still keep animals, and some of them are lucky. But it isn't something we can count on any more, and I'm not going to stand here and tell you it is."

The hall took that quietly. Then somebody near the front called out "Steak!", and the room laughed, and the moment passed.

"When the crew come home, we build the machines. When the machines have done their work, all of this comes out from under the glass and back into the fields. That's the promise." She looked down at the larder, and then up at the cameras, which was where most of her audience was. "A promise, kept in the cold."

In the commentary box, a microphone that should have been off picked up the commentator saying, "That's mine, that is." He sounded pleased.

After the speeches, as before, the director cut back to the larder and held it there. Somewhere between the camera and people's homes the picture caught and stuck: apples, pears, oranges, a heap of rice, a dish of kidney beans, frozen twice over. Nobody watching could tell the difference, and for some time nobody noticed the fault.

Launch +50 years

The stream had started at six, and by seven the counter in the corner of the screen had settled a little above three million. It had replaced the band of sponsors, which had dwindled over the decades to a single line reading Supported by the Mission Trust and then, one year, to nothing. The graphic of the ship was still there, turning slowly, with a caption underneath: Year 50. It had been redrawn twice since the launch, and each time it had come out cleaner and a little less like the ship.

"Good evening," said the commentator, "and welcome to the fiftieth anniversary of the launch." She was young, and she had the voice of someone who had been told to sound warm and had practised. "I should say first that tonight's coverage is dedicated to the man who brought us the very first one. He died in the spring, as many of you will know. He asked us not to make a fuss. We've compromised by mentioning it once."

Behind her the hall was full, though nobody was standing on the benches. There was seating enough for everyone now, and the paint on the benches was three colours deep. Outside, in the car park where the crowd had once stood shoulder to shoulder, there was room to put a blanket down. The three big screens on the outside walls still relayed the ceremony from inside, although the one on the east wall ran a shade warmer than the others. Nobody seemed to mind. When the doors opened the cheer had still been a cheer, but it had gone up from a good many fewer throats than it used to.

In the middle of the hall, on the plinth, stood the larder, the lid down and the light falling through the glass. Apples and pears. Cherries, strawberries, grapes. Oranges. Potatoes, tomatoes. A sheaf of wheat, a cob of sweetcorn, a heap of rice, a dish of kidney beans. At one end, a steak, two pork chops and a coil of sausages. Nothing had moved. Nothing had darkened.

"Fifteen foods," said the commentator, "if you count the meat, and I'm told that you should. Every one of them as fresh as the day it went in."

The Mission Director came to the lectern at eight. He was the fifth to hold the post, and the first for whom it had come with a ribbon of office, which he wore across a dark suit and touched twice before he began, as if to check it was still there. He read from a card.

He began with the names, and the hall answered each of them half a beat behind him, the way people do when they know the responses better than the people.

"Commander Adaeze Schneider. Rafael Castellanos. Ingrid Solheim. Tomasz Wierzbicki. Priya Raman. Kenji Moriyama. Leila Haddad. Callum Reid."

"They sleep a short while," said the Director. "We wait many lifetimes."

"I'm told," said the commentator, over the last of the murmuring, "that the Committee on Ceremony has finally settled the matter of the wording. After eleven sessions it will be when the crew come home, and not if. That will come as a relief to a great many people, and I suspect to the Committee."

"Fifty years ago," said the Director, "we put these things in the cold, and we made a promise over them." He looked at the card, and then, apparently deciding against it, at the hall. "Forty years ago, in this building, my predecessor's predecessor told you that every crop in there was still being grown. I wish I could say the same. Two are not. The strawberry went first, and then the cherry. They are in there, and they are perfect, and they will be grown in neither field nor glasshouse this year."

The hall made a sound that was not quite a groan.

"Ten still are. The apple, the pear, the grape. The orange. The potato and the tomato. The wheat, the sweetcorn, the rice. The kidney bean. Some of them grow under glass now, in the houses. Nobody has built a new house in twenty years, and every year there is less open ground, so more of what we eat needs a roof, and there are only so many roofs. We have had to choose. The houses that grew the strawberry and the cherry now grow wheat and rice. That was a choice, and I would make it again. The meat, as most of you know, we stopped promising a long time ago." He let that settle. "When the crew come home we build the machines. When the machines have done their work, all of this comes out from under the glass and back into the fields. That is the promise. A promise, kept in the cold."

The applause, when it came, was warm and went on rather longer than the speech had earned.

"That line, my producer reminds me, was not the Director's," said the commentator. "It was our late colleague's. He'd have been delighted to hear it still in use. He would also, I imagine, have asked for a credit."

After the speeches the director cut the feed back to the larder and held it there: apples, pears, cherries, strawberries, grapes, oranges, all of it perfect, all of it still. In the corner of the screen the counter read three million, one hundred thousand. It had read three point four at the names. Somebody would be asked about that in the morning, and would say that people had probably gone to bed.

Launch +100 years

The stream began at seven, which was when somebody remembered to start it. There was no director this year, and no commentator. The last commentator had retired, and the post had been allowed to lapse. What there was, was the camera: the one on its bracket above the plinth, which had looked at the larder for a hundred years and continued to send its signal to anyone online who connected to it. The lectern had been set beside the plinth so that the Mission Director would be in shot.

In the corner of the picture the ship sat as a still image. At some point in the last fifty years the animation had been lost, and nobody had known how to restore it, so it hung there with its nose to the left, in the attitude of something that had stopped. Beneath it a caption read Year 100, and beneath that a counter read 412,000. It had read 412,000 since the stream started. Either nobody was arriving and nobody was leaving, or it had stopped counting. Nobody asked.

The hall was full, because it was always full for this. The paint on the benches was four colours deep now. Half the lamps in the roof had failed and nobody had replaced them, so the hall was lit in patches, and by a distance the best-lit object in it was the larder. Near the doors a brass band was playing, though several of the chairs where brass players should have been were empty, and the tune carried on regardless, with a gap in it where the French horn should have been.

Outside, the crowd was small enough that the light from the big screens on the outside walls fell on the tarmac between people. The three screens showed what the stream showed, which was the larder, and the Director's left shoulder. The one on the east wall had developed a pale seam down one side, where the picture did not quite meet itself.

In the middle of the hall stood the larder, the lid down and what light there was falling through the glass. Apples and pears. Cherries, strawberries, grapes. Oranges. Potatoes, tomatoes. A sheaf of wheat, a cob of sweetcorn, a heap of rice, a dish of kidney beans. At one end, a steak, two pork chops and a coil of sausages. Nothing had moved. Nothing had darkened.

The Roll was read by a boy in a borrowed gown. The Reader of the Roll was a post that had not existed fifty years earlier and had, since its inception, acquired a gown. He read from a card.

"Commander Adaeze Schneider. Rafael Castellanos. Ingrid Solheim. Tomasz Wierzbicki."

He stumbled on the fourth, as readers always did, and the front rows supplied it, as they always did.

"Priya Raman. Kenji Moriyama. Leila Haddad. Callum Reid."

The hall murmured each name back to him, in the voice of people saying something they had said a great many times. Nobody cheered, and nobody seemed to think anyone should.

The Mission Director was the ninth to hold the post. She stood half in frame, to the left of the plinth, in the ribbon, and the chain that had since been added to the ribbon, and a robe that had been made for someone taller. She was old, and she read from the card without looking up.

"They sleep a short while," she said. "We wait many lifetimes."

"A hundred years ago we put these things in the cold, and we made a promise over them. Fifty years ago, in this building, you were told that ten of them were still being grown. Now, seven are."

A child said something and was hushed.

"Three of the ten are not. The apple and the pear grew in the houses for as long as we could spare the space, and we can no longer spare it. The potato wants cool ground, and there isn't any. They are in there, and they are perfect." She turned a page that did not need turning. "Nobody has built a new growing house in seventy years. We have had to choose, and the houses that grew the apple and the pear now grow rice. That was a choice, and we would make it again."

She listed what was left, and the camera did not move. "The grape. The orange. The tomato. The wheat, the sweetcorn, the rice. The kidney bean. Seven."

"When the crew come home we build the machines. When the machines have done their work, all of this comes out from under the glass and back into the fields. That is the promise."

"When," murmured the hall, from several places at once.

"A promise," she said, and stopped, as the card told her to.

"Kept in the cold," said the hall, together this time.

The applause was of the kind given to a train that has arrived on time.

Nobody cut back to the larder, because the camera had never left it. The Director stepped out of the frame, and the hall began to empty around the plinth, a few at a time, until the picture was the larder and the patchy lamps and the benches, and in the corner the ship with its nose to the left. The counter read 412,000. It was still reading 412,000 when the last of them had gone.

Launch +250 years

The Keeper knelt at the back of the larder with the grille off and the filter across his knees, and picked the fluff out of it with a bone comb. It was the morning of the ceremony. He had been doing this since he was nine, first beside his father and then instead of him. There was always fluff, and it always came out with the comb, and when it was out the filter went back and the grille went on, and the larder hummed to itself as it always had.

The ceremony took an hour. It had always taken an hour. Nobody knew why, and nobody had asked, but people set off days in advance to be in time for it, some of them, and an hour was what they walked all that way for.

The Roll took five minutes. The speech had taken the rest, once. His grandfather's card had run to both sides, every crop with its house and its prospects and a word on next year. His father's had fitted on one side. His own, which his father had written out for him in the last winter, fitted on half of one. Last night he had read it aloud to the empty hall, timing himself by the drip from the north gutter, and it had taken four minutes.

Last year people had begun leaving before he had finished. They were not rude about it. They simply left, the way people leave a room where nothing more is going to happen.

He picked at a knot of grey with the end of the comb.

The card ended as it had ended in his father's day, and his grandfather's, and before that as far as anyone knew. When the crew come home we build the machines. When the machines have done their work, all of this comes out from under the glass and back into the fields. He had always read it as written. Now, kneeling with the filter, he did the sums as fairly as he could.

Nobody had built a growing house in two hundred years, because nobody could. If the crew walked through the doors this afternoon with the hold full, there would be machines to build and nobody who knew how, and after that the machines would have their work to do, however long that took. He had never seen that written down anywhere. Nobody in the hall tonight would see an orange outside the glass. Neither would their children. That was what the card said, near enough, and he could not see what good it did anyone to hear it once a year.

Hope kept people in the beds. He had seen what happened to a family when it stopped: first they missed a morning, then a week, and then someone would go round and find the door open. Hunger took a long time. Giving up was much quicker.

It would be the same promise, he thought. Only the waiting would be different. And nobody who heard it would live long enough to know the difference.

He put the filter back. He refitted the grille, and laid his hand flat on the casing beside the seam where the lid met the body, which was as close as anyone was meant to come to it. His father had said the same three things every morning they did this together. Clean the filter. Polish the glass. And leave the seal be. You break that before its time and it's all been for nothing. He had said it so often that it had worn smooth.

"Should the seal break early," the Keeper said, to the casing. It needed an answer. "All shall be in vain."

It would do. It would do very well.

He fished a stick of sharpened charcoal out of his pocket and called his daughter over from her sweeping.

The hall was thinner than it had been. It still looked full from the front, but from the plinth the Keeper could see the back benches, where whole rows sat with gaps in them like missing teeth. The lamps in the roof that still worked had been gathered over the middle, so the larder stood in good light and everything else stood in less.

Outside, a few hundred people had come to watch the three big screens on the outside walls, which showed what the camera above the plinth showed, by a cable someone had run out through a window years ago. The first screen worked. The second froze every minute or so and stayed frozen until somebody kicked the wall beneath it, in the one spot that worked. On the third, a band of panels had lost their blue, rendering a broad stripe across the middle of the image the colour of weak tea.

In the middle of the hall stood the larder, the lid down and the light falling through the glass. Apples and pears. Cherries, strawberries, grapes. Oranges. Potatoes, tomatoes. A sheaf of wheat, a cob of sweetcorn, a heap of rice, a dish of kidney beans. At one end, the meat. Nothing had moved. Nothing had darkened.

The Keeper read the Roll himself. There had been a Reader once, but not any more.

"Commander Adaeze Schneider. Rafael Castellanos. Ingrid Solheim. Tomasz Wierzbicki."

The hall gave each name back to him. Nobody stumbled on the fourth now. They had all said it too many times.

"Priya Raman. Kenji Moriyama. Leila Haddad. Callum Reid."

"They sleep a short while," said the Keeper.

"We wait many lifetimes," responded the congregation.

Then the crops. His daughter stood at his elbow with the card, though he did not need it.

"The orange."

"Kept in the cold," said the hall.

"The tomato."

"Kept in the cold."

"The sweetcorn. The rice. The kidney bean."

"Kept in the cold."

"And beyond these walls, not under glass," said the Keeper, "the black-eyed bean, bountiful still."

There was a small sound in the hall at that, which might have been approval. Most of them grew nothing else.

He put his hand on the edge of the plinth.

"When the crew come home," he said, "all of this comes out from under the glass and back into the fields, and onto our tables. All of it. At once."

He had not said the machines. Some of the older ones would notice, he thought. He watched the front rows, and if any of them noticed, they did not seem to mind.

"I will say: Should the seal break early," said the Keeper. "And you will say: All shall be in vain."

The first time was ragged. Half the hall came in late, and the other half didn't come in at all. He said it again, and it was better. The third time they had it, all of them together, and the sound went up into the roof among the dead lamps and came back down.

Outside, the frozen screen caught up with itself a beat behind, and a few hundred voices answered a line that had already been answered.

"A promise," he said.

"Kept in the cold."

Afterwards people stayed. They stood about between the benches and talked, which nobody had done for years, and some of them came up to the plinth to touch the edge of it, and one woman wept and would not say why. The Keeper did not look at the drip from the north gutter. He did not need to.

When they had gone, his daughter swept the aisle, and as she swept she said it under her breath, both halves, the way you say anything you learnt that morning and will say for the rest of your life.

Launch +500 years

They came in the early afternoon, because the ceremony had to be over before the light went, and some of them had a long way to go home.

The big screens were still bolted to the outside walls, three of them, facing what had once been the car park. Nothing had shown on them for longer than anyone could say. Their faces had dulled and begun to peel, and the east one was streaked white beneath the nests along its top.

"They say you used to be able to watch it on those," said an old woman, to nobody in particular.

The child holding her hand looked at the screens, and then at the doors, and then at the few people going in through them.

"Why?" he asked. "Everyone fits inside."

Inside, the temple was lit by whatever came through the roof. That was more than it had been, because the roof had been patched so many times that the patches had begun to come away from one another, and the light came down in long, dusty columns between them. Where the rain came down too, there were buckets, and there had been one under the north gutter for as long as there had been a Keeper.

The few hundred filled the front of the hall and left the rest to the dust. They sat close together, the way people do when there is more room than they need.

The Keeper was on the ladder when they arrived. It leant against the pillar beneath the camera's bracket, and he was at the top of it with a cloth, rubbing at the glass in front of the lens, slowly, with his tongue between his teeth. Beneath the lens a small light glowed, the only artificial light in the building that nobody had to carry. When he was satisfied he came down, rung by rung, and laid the ladder along the wall where it always lay.

In the middle of the temple stood the larder, the lid down and the light falling through the glass. Apples and pears. Cherries, strawberries, grapes. Oranges. Potatoes, tomatoes. A sheaf of wheat, a cob of sweetcorn, a heap of rice, a dish of kidney beans. At one end, the meat. Nothing had moved. Nothing had darkened.

The Keeper read the Roll.

"Commander Adaeze Schneider. Rafael Castellanos. Ingrid Solheim. Tomasz Wierzbicki."

The hall gave the names back to him, a beat behind. There were fewer voices than names would have liked.

"Priya Raman. Kenji Moriyama. Leila Haddad. Callum Reid."

"They sleep a short while," said the Keeper.

"We wait many lifetimes," said the hall.

Someone near the back coughed and did not stop for some time.

"Beyond these walls, not under glass," said the Keeper, "the black-eyed bean. Bountiful still."

"Kept in the cold," said the hall.

Nobody looked at the larder when they said it. There was no reason to. They knew what was in it, and they knew what was in the fields, and they had never needed the two to be the same.

"When the crew come home," said the Keeper, "all of this comes out from under the glass and back into the fields, and onto our tables. All of it. At once."

"When?" lamented a man, not loudly.

It was not part of the words. It was a question, and the people near him looked at their hands until it had gone.

The Keeper waited, as the card told him to, and then a little longer.

"Should the seal break early," he said.

"All shall be in vain," said the hall. It came in pieces, some voices ahead and some behind, and a few didn't come at all. He said it again, because the card said to, and it was no better. The third time he didn't wait for the answer to finish.

"A promise."

"Kept in the cold."

Then they went, because the light was going. They didn't stay to talk. A few touched the edge of the plinth on the way past, the way you would touch a doorframe in a house you were leaving. The old woman went with the child, and the child looked back once at the dead screens and then didn't look again.

The Keeper emptied the bucket under the north gutter and put it back. The columns of light leaned further and further across the floor, then went thin, then went out. In the dark, only the small light under the lens remained, and the camera went on looking at the larder for anybody who might want to see it.

The building had been a growing house once. Most of its glass had gone long before either of them was born, and what was left hung in the frame in sharp grey teeth, so that the sun came in wherever it liked. The beds ran the length of it, raised and edged with stone. In the nearest one the black-eyed bean plants stood where they had been put in, brown to the root, their pods flat and empty as paper.

Boy and Girl sat against the far wall, where a stretch of ancient brick still kept off the wind. They had been sitting there for most of three days, and for most of three days they had been having the same conversation.

"Say it again," said Boy.

"There's food in the temple."

"There's not food. There's the larder."

"There's food in the larder."

He didn't answer that. He hadn't answered it yesterday either.

Girl looked at the bed. "Nothing came up," she said. "Nothing's going to come up. We had the last of the dried ones on…" She stopped, because she had lost count of the days. "We had the last of them."

"I know."

"So say what we do instead."

He looked at the bean plants for a long while. "We wait."

"For what?"

"For them." He didn't say who. Nobody had ever needed to say who. "They sleep a short while. We wait many lifetimes."

"Ours are about used up."

"When they come home, it all comes back. All of it, all at once. That's what he says."

"That's what he says," said Girl. "And who's it going to come back to?"

Boy said nothing.

"Count," she said.

"Don't."

"Count. There was Ama, and then Ama's brother, and then your dad. Now there's us, and there's him. Count."

"Three."

"And when it's two? And then one?" She pulled her knees up, slowly, because everything was slow now. "They come home, and there's him, on his own, polishing the glass. And then there isn't even him. They put it there for us. That's what it's for. It's for the people who are waiting, and we're who's waiting. If we die waiting, there was never any point to it. There was never any point to them."

Boy picked at a thread on his sleeve. "Should the seal break early," he said, and stopped. He had meant it as an argument. It had come out as the start of something, the way it always did, and there was nobody to say the rest.

Girl didn't say the rest.

"It's been six hundred years," she said. "How is that early?"

He had no answer to that, and she didn't wait for one.

The temple was not far. From the gap where the door had been you could see its roof, patched so many times that where it wasn't holes, it was more patch than roof. When they were small it had been a run of a few minutes across the cracked yard and past the water tank. Now it was half an hour, with a rest at the tank and another at the steps.

"He won't let us," said Boy. "You know he won't."

"He might not even be…"

"He is. I saw him yesterday, on the steps, emptying the bucket."

"Then he'll be inside."

"He's always inside." Boy rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. "We tell him, and he'll say it. The whole thing. Then he'll stand in front of it."

"He's as old as the walls, and he's as hungry as we are. He can barely carry the bucket."

"He'll still stand in front of it."

Girl was quiet for a long time. The sun moved a hand's width along the beds.

"Then we move him," she said.

"And if he won't be moved?"

She looked at him then, properly, the way she hadn't for most of the three days. "Then we'll have to hurt him."

Boy looked down at his hands. They were not hands that could hurt anybody much. Neither were hers.

"You'd do that," he said.

"I'd rather not do any of it." She nodded at the bed, at the paper pods. "But I'd rather that than this."

"Do you even know how to open it?"

"No."

"Then…"

"We'll find out."

The wind found a pane somewhere in the roof and made it tick against its frame. Boy listened to it for a while.

"All right," he said.

"All right what?"

"All right. If he tries to stop us…"

He didn't finish it, and this time she didn't need him to.

Standing took them both a while.

If anybody had been watching the stream, they would have seen the larder, from above and a little to one side, as it had always been seen. The white casing, and the glass panel in the lid, and through the glass the food: apples and pears, cherries, strawberries, grapes, oranges, potatoes, tomatoes, the wheat, the sweetcorn, the rice, and nearest the lens a dish of kidney beans, dark red against the white. Around the plinth, a margin of floor. Across the floor, the slow columns of daylight from the roof.

And they would have heard, close by and a little above, a small regular squeak, like a cloth on glass.

A door, somewhere out of frame. The squeak stopped.

Footsteps followed, slow and uneven, with long gaps between them. Twice they stopped altogether, and there was breathing, and then they began again. Two heads entered the bottom of the frame: the tops of them, the hair thin, the scalps showing through. Two pairs of shoulders that a coat would have hung from as if from a hook. They came on until they stood at the edge of the plinth, one either side of the near corner, and there they stopped and looked down at the larder for a long time.

Then one of them reached out, and a hand lay flat on the glass.

"What are you doing?"

The weak voice came from close to the camera, above it and behind. The two of them flinched, both at once, and looked up towards it, straight out of the picture. Two faces, all bone and eye, searching for whoever had spoken. They found nobody. For a moment neither of them moved.

"Should the seal break early," said the voice.

It waited, as the card told it to.

Nobody answered. The two faces went down again, back to the larder, and the hand that had been on the glass went to the edge of the lid and felt along it, looking for something to take hold of.

Above the frame, the ladder rattled against the pillar. There was a quick, scraping scramble of feet on rungs, too quick, and the voice again, higher now, answering itself: "All shall…"

He fell into the top of the frame all at once: an old man in a grey robe, a shape with its arms out. His head struck the corner of the larder, where the lid met the casing, with a sound like a dropped pot, and then he was on the floor at the foot of the plinth, at the edge of the frame.

He breathed out once. Then once more, shallower. Then nothing.

On the corner of the larder, across the seam between the lid and the casing, there was a smear of dark red, wet and bright against the white. It was the first mark anyone had ever made on it.

The other two looked at him. One of them, the taller, looked a little longer than the other, and made a sound in his throat, low and short, the sound of someone setting down something heavy. Then he turned back to the larder.

The other was already moving. She went along the side of the plinth and round the end of it, out of the frame, and there was a pause, and the soft sound of hands patting along a casing.

There was a solid click, and then a run of smaller, busier sounds as the latches let it go. After several seconds the lid of the pod rose under its own power, smoothly, without hurry. The seal came away from the rim cleanly, all the way round, narrow parts and all. It took half the red up with it and left half on the casing. It went past upright and leaned out over the floor, over the place where the old man lay, until all that showed of him beneath it was one hand, palm up, at the very edge of the frame. Cold came out of the pod like breath on a winter morning, rolled over the rim and spilled down the sides, thinned, and was gone. It was the coldest air either of them would ever feel.

There before them lay the pristine foods, most of which their great-grandparents had only ever seen here, secured behind glass. Now, the glass was gone.

She came back into the frame. They stood at the open side, the two of them, shoulder to shoulder, and for a while they only looked. One of them made a small noise that might have been the beginning of a laugh. His hand went out, stopped, went out again.

He reached first, for the beans: fatter and redder than any they had ever grown, but beans all the same. As soon as his fingers touched them they slumped into a grey sludge that ran across the dish and over its lip and spread along the bottom of the pod.

He took his hand back and looked at it.

Then, more quickly, as though speed might make a difference, he reached for an apple. It went the same way. Something rose out of the pod then, a smell that made both of them turn their heads, though neither of them stepped back.

She took a potato, and it was nothing in her hand. She reached for the oranges beside it, and they went too, one and then the next, and she kept reaching after there was any point, until she stopped.

"Oh," he said.

She didn't look at him. Under her breath, so quietly that the camera barely had it, she said, "All shall be in vain."

They slumped down against the open pod, first her and then him, slowly, the way everything was done now, beneath the red on the casing and the grey settling in the bottom of the pod. Neither of them spoke again. Neither of them moved much.

The camera held them there, as it had held everything else, and it was still holding them when the light faded.
