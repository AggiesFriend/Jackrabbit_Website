The World

Nothing in it is broken. That is rather the trouble.

Spoiler-light: this page describes the world of Trust Us as you meet it in the opening chapters. The rest, the book would prefer to show you itself.

Where you are from

The Enclaves

Generations after the world got smaller, people live in enclaves: fenced settlements with co-op stores, patched roofs, and gates that stand open in daylight. Ashburn, where Daniel and Ruth live, is one of the better ones — it has a working data centre, seed-tables to check, and a barber, if you save up.

Life in an enclave is not desperate. It is merely careful. Wages are counted a fortnight at a time, everything that can be mended is mended, and every household keeps its sums — because the things worth having are expensive, and the things worth having most of all are licences. What a licence buys, the enclaves do not much discuss with strangers. They save for them all the same, in passbooks kept in drawers, in two lines of careful handwriting: his and hers.

How you get anywhere

The Road

There are no aeroplanes and no engines to speak of. There are carts, and horses, and sailing ships crewed by people who have made the same crossing so many times the choreography is second nature. A journey from Ashburn to England takes the better part of two months, if the weather holds, and the weather is under no obligation to hold.

The road shows you what the world has become. Settlements gone quiet, with good wells and nobody left to mind them. Coastlines that stop being coastlines at low tide, when the sea uncovers what it keeps: rooflines, car parks, a street sign leaning at an angle no wind ever put it at. And moving through all of it, unhurried and unbothered, the couriers — men like Ray Tanner, who carry sealed cases from data centre to data centre, wear weapons more valuable than everything else on the cart, and fill in an audit form for every round fired.

Spares out. Dead equipment back. Every run that has ever been made.

Where the answers come from

Slough — The Bank

Everybody knows what Slough is. The safest enclave there is, by every account, because it is built around the one thing everybody in the world still needs. Clean water on tap. Working streetlights. Warmth that comes from nowhere in particular and has evidently been there a long time. And at the heart of it, the institution nobody calls by its proper name: the Bank.

When an enclave has a problem too hard to solve — a harvest that must be sequenced across fifty-one settlements, a river on the wrong side of a lost boundary — it sends a delegation, and the delegation pays the fee, and the Bank delivers. Its people are kind, and organised, and proud of what the place is for. Its floors are clean. Its filing is immaculate. Its promises are precise:

Notice of intake

Problem class: as declared · Fee: per the schedule of rates

You'll have an answer by the spring after next.

That's not a promise about the answer. That's a promise about the date.

How the Bank produces its answers is not a secret, exactly. It is something better than a secret: a thing it has never occurred to anybody to ask.

Where the evenings go

The Estate

Somewhere else entirely, there is an estate: grey blocks under a grey sky, a drizzle that never quite arrives and never quite stops, work that leaves nothing to carry home, and neighbours nobody has ever properly met. The days there are not lived so much as completed.

The evenings are another matter. The evenings belong to the games — bright, warm, loud with cheerful noise, the one thing in anybody's life with colour in it. There are market seasons and farming valleys, guilds and first clears, and above it all the boards: your name, and everybody else's, and how high yours sits. Prizes come and go — hats, cloaks, the rare box that simply appears on a balcony six floors up — but the board never goes grey. Everybody looks. Everybody but Vernon.

And once in a long while, a name vanishes from the columns overnight, and the estate raises a toast, because everybody knows what that means: promoted. Lifted clean off the board and out of the grey for good. Where the promoted go, nobody can say. It is simply the thing that happens to the ones who climb high enough.

Beyond the last of the flats, past the kerb where the pavement stops, there is nothing at all — just pale, dry dirt under the falling drizzle, going away flat in every direction. Almost nothing. Out where nobody has ever had a reason to walk, there is a cave with a grid cut into its floor, twelve stones that sit one to a cell, and an old man who will be delighted to meet you.

Every single time.
